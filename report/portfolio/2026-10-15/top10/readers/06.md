## 原文正文

Seek Inspiration, Not Imitation

Self-play RL with Bayesian hidden-hand estimation and per-matchup policy switching — 6th place (Slowking / Dragapult ex Dusknoir)

The Pokémon Company - PTCG AI Battle Challenge Strategy

Hackathon Writeup · Sep 10, 2026

fullscreen
1. Overview

Our agent's policy was trained by self-play reinforcement learning alone: games between copies of itself, with no human or leaderboard play as teacher. Making that work with hidden hands, delayed effects and long decision sequences needed four supporting pieces (§2, Fig.1): a two-stage self-play pipeline yielding one policy per opponent archetype, a Bayesian estimate of the opponent's hidden cards, incremental observation updates that reorder card-effect processing so credit lands on the actual choice, and pre-computed damage/KO features with card-agnostic effect representations. Evidence comes on three levels (§3–4): a 6th-place finish and its rating; playing quality with matchups controlled, plus robustness when any matchup, initial state or situation is removed; and micro-level play quality from every leaderboard decision.

Fig.1 One decision at inference; all inputs derive from the engine observation.

2. Approach
2.1 Why reinforcement learning

Imitation learning is bounded by the existence and level of its exemplar; self-play RL learns from reward and needs none. Our PPO policy selects among the engine's legal actions, playing random initial boards against a copy of itself, so a wide variety of openings and prize situations are visited and reward maximized from each.

"No imitation" refers to the play policy; deck building and meta reading stayed human work, and leaderboard logs served only for opponent decklists, archetype statistics, an action-evidence model (§2.3) and our card choices (§4). The difficulty: reward is sparse (±1 result, ±0.1 per prize) over 67 (Slowking) or 92 (Dragapult) decisions per game, some cards knock out their own user or act with delay, and the opponent's hand is hidden; §2.4 answers the first two, §2.3 the third.

2.2 Two-stage self-play and per-matchup weight switching

Stage 1 trains one policy per archetype by self-play within that deck: about 18 archetypes plus two "general" runs pooling rare archetypes (Fig.2). Stage 2 fine-tunes the policy of each submitted deck against each opponent archetype's pool of real decklists, so all 32 archetypes are covered by 18–20 policies per deck (the rarest share one). At inference the archetype posterior (§2.3) selects the policy; until it is confident, two or three decisions in, we act on the posterior-weighted average of every policy's action probabilities.

In a pre-submission test, replacing the general policy with one specialized for the opponent raised the win rate against that opponent by about 13 pt (Table 1). Adding an archetype is cheap: Sylveon appeared in five leaderboard decks on August 15; on August 16, the submission deadline, we appended it to the statistics and trained a policy against it for both decks; both then won at least 80% of their leaderboard games against it (n=20, 19). Across all 2,775 leaderboard games the same 60 cards follow a different plan per opponent, e.g. the Dragapult deck builds its Dusknoir line in 97% of games against Crustle but 15% against Slowking (Fig.10).

Fig.2 How the per-opponent policies are produced.

2.3 Bayesian estimation of hidden information

Beyond tracking public zones, we estimate the expected copies of every card in the opponent's hidden zones (Fig.3) and feed them to the network as per-card expected-count inputs; because the input is per card, an unexpected card or new archetype shifts the distribution without changing the input shape.

Layer 1 is an archetype posterior: prior = each archetype's leaderboard game count; evidence = the most copies of each card seen at once in public zones (a lower bound on the copies in the deck, so absence is never evidence); likelihood = leaderboard copy-count distributions with Dirichlet shrinkage, giving conditional expected copies per card. Layer 2 allocates them to hand, deck and prizes with a filter: mass moves under draws, plays and face-down moves, is renormalized to the public hand count, and receives negative evidence from actions ("could have used it, did not") via a small MLP fitted on 4.5 M leaderboard rows. Top-1 archetype accuracy is 99% by the second turn. Giving the same policy no estimate of the hidden cards, only public zone counts, lowers the win rate (Table 1).

Fig.3 How the opponent's hidden cards are estimated at each decision.

2.4 Designs derived from the game's structure

Prizes are the win condition. Win/loss is one bit for dozens of decisions, so each prize taken or lost adds ±0.1 against ±1 for the result; the winner is credited with the loser's remaining prizes, so shaping never contradicts the result.

Some effects resolve in several steps. For delayed and multi-target effects (e.g. Punk Up, Oil Salvo) the observation is updated incrementally and effect processing is reordered into the order the player chooses. Cursed Blast goes further: the engine knocks out the user first and asks for the target afterwards, so the self-KO step alone would absorb the loss and be unlearned; we merge the two into one decision, so cost and gain are judged together and it rarely fires without a worthwhile target (§4.2).

Multi-card choices are sequences of single picks. We split "Do X to up to N cards" into one-card decisions: the remaining budget is written into the observation, each pick's result is applied to the board before the next, and a STOP action handles "up to" (e.g. Xerosic's Machinations, Punk Up).

Damage is deterministic. Damage and KOs are fixed by the rules, so they are simulated and supplied as candidate features, which lowers the learning cost.

Cards share structure. The policy is a Transformer whose input tokens are the cards on the board: each token combines the card's id, an 87-dimensional effect tag extracted from the simulator, and its state (damage, energy, position), so cards with similar effects share most of their representation (Fig.4).

Actions are defined by the cards they touch. A pointer head scores each legal action by gathering the board tokens it refers to (Fig.4). The output is one score per legal action rather than a softmax over a fixed catalogue of actions, so no probability mass is spent on illegal actions, and a new card needs no new output unit.

Fig.4 Policy network (0.73 M params): every card is a token, hidden zones enter as expected-count tokens, legal actions gather their board tokens and are scored by a pointer head.

2.5 Hypotheses tested

Table 1 lists what we tried: three adopted, four rejected. The criterion was win rate against a fixed opponent in A/B tests of 600–6,480 games, with the same checkpoint's self-play (50%) as baseline.

3. Evaluation
3.1 Rank

We finished 6th. The 6-hour moving-average rating had median 1239 (p10–p90 1209–1278) for the Slowking deck (A) and 1178 (1147–1203) for the Dragapult deck (B); final official rating 1229.5 (Fig.6).

3.2 Piloting gain: playing quality with matchups controlled

Piloting gain = leaderboard win rate against archetype X minus the win rate in our own arena against a sparring opponent co-trained for X (the x axis of Fig.5). Since sparring opponents were trained against our policies, positive gain means the leaderboard's pilots of X are weaker than ours. For A the gain is positive in 7 of 8 matchups with n≥15 (sign test p=0.035): game-weighted local expectation 49.8% versus leaderboard 54.6%, +4.9 pt (SE 1.4). For B it is neutral, +0.8 pt. Decomposing leaderboard win rate ≈ 50 + matchup + piloting (an approximation): A ≈ 50 − 0.2 + 4.9, so its rating comes from play, not the meta; B ≈ 50 + 1.9 + 0.8, weighed down by the Dragapult. During development the gain was a diagnostic rather than a report card: a matchup that diverged usually pointed to an implementation gap, while one that did not diverge confirmed a genuine deck-level disadvantage. This comparison is not clean: decklists differ, the sparring partner is co-trained to beat our policy, and it compares pilots, not other teams' methods.

Fig.5 Leaderboard versus local win rate per opponent archetype (size = games, 95% intervals); above the diagonal our pilot beat the leaderboard's. The ~100 games against unlisted low-rated opponents are excluded.

3.3 Consistency over repeated games

Over the 15-day evaluation performance was stationary and stayed within the winning ranks: A was above the 8th-place line 82% of the time, B above the 23rd-place line 96%, and the team (max of the two) in the top 8 for 84% (Fig.6). Across 2,775 games (A 1,395 / B 1,380) no games were lost to errors or timeouts.

Fig.6 Leaderboard rating (6-hour moving average) with team-level 8th- and 23rd-place lines (dashed); 08-17 to 08-20 reconstructed by inverting the Elo updates.

3.4 No reliance on particular initial states, matchups, or situations

Initial states. First versus second differs by 5.7 pt (A) and 3.8 pt (B) against 5.4 pt in the population, both win going second, and mulligan games are won at or above the population rate (Fig.7, right).

Matchups. We ask whether the rating survives removing any one matchup (performance rating excluding it, Fig.7). A's deck is matchup-neutral for the opponents it met (§3.2). Excluding any archetype leaves A within 1245–1259; excluding the three largest contributors together (Dragapult, Slowking, Solrock, 63% of games) still gives 1230, a top-8 rating. B's largest single exclusion moves it 10 points (Slowking, 1181 → 1171), comparable to A's 7, so B does not depend on any favourable matchup either. Excluding the Dragapult, its worst matchup (34% of games, Δ −9.5), instead raises it to 1223: one structural weakness (§4.1). Against a deck absent from the training pool (Bronzong/Munkidori) A scored 72% (n=18) and B 50% (n=26); 98% of games were known matchups, so this is our only evidence on unknowns.

Situations. B wins 48% of games in which it loses the first prize (population 38%) and recovers from a two-prize deficit 1.4× as often as the population; A, a burst deck, instead reaches a two-prize lead in 65% of games (population 51%).

Fig.7 Robustness: per-matchup result against Elo expectation (left), performance rating with any one archetype removed (middle), and win rate by initial state and prize situation against the population (right).

4. Deck
4.1 Why these two decks

Two criteria (decklists in Fig.8): hard for other teams to counter, i.e. hard to train a sparring partner for, and weaknesses that do not overlap. Slowking is a combo deck: place a Pokémon with a costly but powerful attack on top of the deck, then Seek Inspiration uses that attack without paying its cost. Under random play the combo rarely occurs yet its return is large, so we expected it to be hard to learn by RL and hard to reproduce; our self-play agent learned it (§4.2) and wins 58% of mirrors against the leaderboard's Slowking pilots (Fig.5). Dragapult is a midrange deck: Dragapult ex's Phantom Dive, powerful but demanding in energy and damage-counter management, plus Dusclops/Dusknoir's Cursed Blast to disrupt, finish and remove counter-cards. Placing counters with later turns in mind and pricing Cursed Blast, which costs a prize of one's own, are hard to evaluate, so again hard to reproduce; our agent does (Fig.9), and no other top team ran the Dusknoir variant. The Dragapult rows in Fig.5 and Fig.7 are therefore against the plain variant. The weaknesses differ: Alakazam and Hydrapple for Slowking, the plain Dragapult for Dragapult (Fig.7), so whichever came to dominate the meta, one deck would hold (Fig.5).




Fig.8 Decklists of the submitted decks. Card images are taken unmodified from the competition-provided Card List.

4.2 Key cards: choice and use

Card choices followed the meta: Lillie's Clefairy ex for the Dragapult-heavy field (Slowking deck), Moltres for Hydrapple (Dragapult deck). Seek Inspiration, the card behind the title, was used 2,793 times and found a usable attacker on top in 93.4% of them, against ~25% for an untouched deck: the agent sets this up with Academy at Night and Ciphermaniac's Codebreaking and picks the borrowed attack by the opponent (Fig.9, Fig.10). Cursed Blast fires from Dusclops (50 damage) in only 24.5% of games it reaches the board but from Dusknoir (130) in 86.3%, and 83.0% of its targets are knocked out that turn; a 320-HP Dragapult ex is softened for Phantom Dive to finish (89.4% within the turn), so the self-KO is paid for by a KO. Clefairy is played in 88% of games where the opponent shows Dragapult ex and 29% otherwise, Moltres in 89% against Hydrapple/Arboliva and 25% otherwise: the agent uses each card in the matchup it was added for (Fig.9).
