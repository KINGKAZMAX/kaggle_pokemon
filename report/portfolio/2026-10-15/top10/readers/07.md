7th Place Solution for the PTCG AI Battle Challenge Simulation Competition

Reading the Cards: a Deck-Agnostic Policy Trained by a League of Its Own Exploiters

The Pokémon Company - PTCG AI Battle Challenge Strategy

Hackathon Writeup · Sep 13, 2026

fullscreen

We thank The Pokémon Company, HEROZ, Matsuo Institute and Kaggle for this wonderful challenge.

Overview

The model is a ~10.7M-parameter encoder-decoder transformer with a GRU belief core, conditioned on card text. Every legal action is encoded from its card text and live state, scored by a pointer head and softmaxed over the options that exist: no fixed action space and no card-identity parameters, so the same network pilots any 60-card list. Trained by reinforcement learning against a league of 70+ frozen opponents trained to beat it, it acts greedily at inference, except for a deterministic lethal check and a mask on dominated actions.

The Deck: Dragapult / Blaziken

We chose Dragapult from day one: familiar, strong, decision-dense. Our hypothesis: such a deck leaves the most room for a trained policy to exceed human play. We started on Dragapult/Dusknoir and switched to Blaziken mid-competition as Grass decks spread in the meta. The list implies a plan, engine first, Blaziken after, which the agent learned unprompted: over 151 final games the first Drakloak came on its second turn and Blaziken ex on its fourth, with two Drakloaks already down in 99 of 128 games.

The ladder meta: Dragapult, Alakazam, Grass decks, Slowking, Mega Kangaskhan/Crustle, Mega Lucario, Grimmsnarl.

A detail we liked: the agent often held Dragapult back from the Active, as if waiting to draw one of those two cards.
Model Architecture

The encoder reads the board as 38 words, each a row of 1145 numbers through a shared MLP plus a learned role embedding, then eight transformer layers (d_model 256, 4 heads, feed-forward 512). The decoder encodes each legal action as a row of 1079 numbers plus a learned action-plane embedding and cross-attends to the board over eight more layers. Role and plane are the only learned identity tables.

A card enters as text, in five blocks:

block	what it holds
printed text	card name and rules text, frozen all-mpnet-base-v2 embedding, as in Cardsformer [3]
effects	counts per category of effect type, target, unit, modifier and trigger, plus value sum and max per unit. Atoms parsed once offline by an LLM from the engine's own text and numbers over a 72-type vocabulary, with a verifier pass and an engine check: 0 of 986 base damages altered
printed stats	type, HP, retreat, stage, energy type, weakness, resistance, ex/Tera, ability, damage and cost of each attack
live state	HP left and maximum, attached energy by type, tool, whether it entered play this turn and so cannot evolve yet
position	on the player word only: our energies by type in deck, discard and known prizes, and our cards by class still in deck

A GRU (hidden 256), ByteRL's recurrent core [1], reads the board summary with per-zone means and carries a belief vector across the game to three places: a token in the decoder's cross-attention memory, the pointer's query, and every legal action's score. We measured that wiping the belief's past while keeping the current board changes 5.9% of 10,109 decisions. League win rates edged up after it went in, though we never ran a controlled test.

Scoring is a pointer, query against action key, DI-star's target head [4]. The value head is distributional, 51 bins with HL-Gauss targets [5], since with hidden cards an outcome may be better modelled as a distribution.

Training is IMPALA-style V-trace [6] with UPGO [2] inside a PPO clip of 0.2 and an entropy bonus of 0.01. AdamW, lr 6e-5, batches of 6 full-game trajectories, one pass. A training-only auxiliary head reads the chosen action's token and predicts what the engine logged: which of 13 effect types occurred (damage, counters, healing, energy, knockout) and their sizes (weight 0.15). The reward is the outcome alone, +1 or −1.

Inputs Instead of Rules

When the policy played badly, we looked for a quantity it could not see rather than a rule to impose. Hard-coded rules made it worse; exposing the missing quantities as inputs made it better. Each action row carries a tail of engine-computed numbers: energy still missing after an attach, damage counters left to a knockout and the prize it pays, which energy unlocks which attack, damage after weakness and resistance. The line that added the knockout distance gained 4.3 points of win rate over its pre-feature checkpoint, 1,500 games per arm (z=2.5).

The second lever was the opponents: a new opponent deck forces the agent to find new lines in its own deck. One example: Crustle takes no damage from Pokémon ex, so Phantom Dive does nothing to it; in the league it forced the main to learn to attack with Chi-Yu.

Lethal Search and Masks

The lethal finder runs a depth-first search over move combinations to prove the game can be closed this turn. Lethal detection is computation, not strategy, so it overrides the policy. We distilled it too: a line the engine proves wins this turn is always correct, so unlike a hand-written rule it adds no error, and imitating it teaches the network how several moves combine.

A mask removes a few actions we judged dominated, such as damage counters on a Pokémon already at 0 HP or Fezandipiti ex while its draw ability is inactive; the network chooses among the rest. This contradicts our own view of hand-written rules: we accepted small errors in the network in exchange for removing visible blunders.

Behavioural Cloning

We used no behavioural cloning: ladder-replay cloning was measured and dropped. Seeding from the main's weights, shown below, learned a new deck faster than any clone.
Only the very first network was cloned, from rule-based Dragapult and Mega Lucario agents, as a warm start for reinforcement learning.

Training: 110 Opponents For One League

A league inspired by AlphaStar [2]: no mirror against its current self, only its frozen past checkpoints.

The league is 72 frozen opponents, each pinned to the deck it learned on; 87% of games go to them, 13% to the main's past checkpoints.
Sampling is PFSP, opponents weighted by loss rate. Every ~40 minutes all are reweighted, w = 0.15 + (1 - winrate)^2; an opponent beaten ≥ 76% of the time is absorbed: weight zero, still measured.
Handed a deck it had never seen, the main learned to pilot it in hours. For every meta archetype we trained a dedicated exploiter: a copy of the main's latest weights piloting that archetype's deck, 80% against the frozen main, 20% against the league. The copy opens near 4% because it cannot yet use those cards, but it knows the game and nothing in the architecture is tied to a deck, so it climbs fast: meganium_ogerpon went from 4.0% to 75.0% in 8 hours, about 34M decisions, and at peak we added three opponents a day.
Three exploiters on decks the main had never played, submitted after a short training run, peaked in the top 50 of the public ladder, one in the top 30. We trained 110 exploiters; the league held about 72.

Scale: The farm was a set of game generators shipping trajectories to a single learner on one RTX 4090. Training the main consumed about 3.6 billion decisions, some 35 million games. The bottleneck was game generation, not the learner; what moved it most was a vectorised generator batching many games per forward pass.

Robustness to card substitution: Mid-training we swapped Torchic and Combusken for their versions from a different set, different HP and attacks, same role. The main used them at once. The checkpoint saved before the swap played the new list against the old in an 800-game A/B: 51.6% [48.2, 55.1].

Results on the Simulation Ladder

The submitted agent finished 7th of 6,807 teams at 1226.6; a twin checkpoint 350 iterations earlier closed at 1211.9.

Over its last 1,000 ladder games it won 57.4%: 88.5% outside the top 23, 51.3% against the top 23. It is stable: four blocks of 250 games read 58.0, 56.8, 56.4, 58.4; by seat, 57.9% as seat 0, which chooses who starts, and 56.9% as seat 1.
We met 21 of the top 23 teams at least 15 times: 50% or better against 16, under 40% against three.

On the final leaderboard scale the same frozen agent's rank fluctuates around a median of 8th, extremes 2nd and 22nd.

Important Weaknesses Found During the Evaluation Period

Even though it reads the cards, the agent cannot change strategy against an unseen deck, and against some decks the outcome is a step function of whether it knows the counter-strategy. A Bronzong list uses Evolution Jammer every turn, so nothing evolves from hand; the agent waits instead of removing the 110 HP Bronzong.
A Sylveon list uses Safeguard, immune to damage from Pokémon ex, and the agent attacks into it for zero until it decks out. In a local probe on that replay it stopped wasting attacks when we swapped Crustle in for Sylveon, same ability text. It had learned Crustle as a whole, name, stats and text, not the consequence the two cards share. Text alone did not transfer here; such effects need an action's consequence as an explicit input. A candidate fix, untested and our first item of future work: resolve every candidate attack on the engine and expose the resulting damage, zero against Safeguard.

What Did Not Work
what we tried	outcome
larger networks	a 5.7× wider net took far longer to train and, given the same time, did not play better; going deeper saturated at 8 layers, two thirds of decisions settled after the first
hand-written rules and mechanical teachers	every one we tested cost win rate, by up to 36 points. Our reading: a rule covers only the cases we thought of, and training writes the rest into the weights as error
search in training	search used to generate or label training games gave nothing over greedy and was heavy on our hardware; energy-only search read 5 points above greedy, within noise
