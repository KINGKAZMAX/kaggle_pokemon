[8th Place Solution] Yaruki Genki Miwa Haruki: Generalist-to-Specialist PPO

273 million games of GPU-accelerated self-play, expert replay reviews, and six matchup specialists behind our Dragapult ex agent

The Pokémon Company - PTCG AI Battle Challenge Strategy

Hackathon Writeup · Sep 14, 2026

Overview

Our team, “Yaruki Genki Miwa Haruki” (やる気元気ミワハルキ), finished 8th in the Simulation track of Kaggle's Pokémon TCG AI Battle competition. We paired Dragapult ex's flexible Prize-taking with a Transformer policy trained through behavior cloning (BC) and large-scale PPO self-play. We then trained matchup specialists to address weaknesses identified by fixed-opponent evaluations and expert replay reviews.

Deck Choice

We chose Dragapult ex to reduce dependence on particular matchups; Figure 1 shows our submitted list. With the final opponent distribution uncertain, we prioritized flexible routes to 6 Prizes over exploiting a favorable matchup.

Figure 1 | Submitted deck: 19 Pokémon, 32 Trainers, and 9 Energy cards.

Dragapult's 320 HP and lack of Weakness help limit opposing knockouts. Phantom Dive deals 200 damage to the Active Pokémon and distributes 6 damage counters across the Bench. Repeated attacks can take down high-HP Pokémon while accumulating Bench damage, or knock out multiple low-HP Pokémon. These routes to efficient Prize exchanges make target selection and damage-counter allocation central policy decisions.

Our support cards serve 3 objectives: disruption, attack support, and comeback routes (Table 1).

Table 1 | Support-card roles.

Objective	Key cards	Role
Deny opposing Prizes	Unfair Stamp, Judge, Crushing Hammer, Budew	Disrupt hands or Energy and restrict Item use.
Improve Prize-taking	Crispin, Meowth ex, Risky Ruins, Munkidori, Boss's Orders	Maintain attacks, place or move damage counters, and select targets.
Preserve comeback routes	Moltres	Add a 1-Prize attacker against Pokémon ex, including Fire-weak targets.

Risky Ruins places damage counters on newly Benched non-Darkness Basic Pokémon on both sides. Munkidori moves ours onto opposing Pokémon, turning a shared drawback into offense while removing damage from our board. Boss's Orders pulls damaged targets into the Active Spot for Phantom Dive's 200 damage (Figure 2).

Figure 2 | Dragapult's attack and support interactions. Card conditions apply, including Darkness Energy for Munkidori.

Crispin helps meet Phantom Dive's Fire–Psychic Energy requirement: Ultra Ball can find Meowth ex, whose Ability searches for Crispin. Together with normal attachment, Crispin can power an unenergized Dragapult in 1 turn (Figure 3). Crushing Hammer removes opposing Energy on a successful coin flip, helping delay attacks and protect Dragapult from one-hit knockouts.

Figure 3 | Search, Energy supply, and disruption, subject to each card's use conditions.

Moltres addresses the Hydrapple ex and Raging Bolt ex matchups that challenged us on the leaderboard. For 1 Fire Energy, it deals 110 damage to Pokémon ex, or 220 against Fire-weak Teal Mask Ogerpon ex. Taking 2 Prizes while conceding 1 provides a comeback route when Dragapult is being knocked out in 1 attack (Figure 4).

Figure 4 | Moltres's damage thresholds and Prize exchanges. The attack counts assume the depicted single knockouts.

Evolving Drakloak into Dragapult ex trades Recon Directive's draw for offense. We therefore included 1 Dunsparce and 1 Dudunsparce, its evolved form, to maintain access to disruption and attack resources. Run Away Draw draws 3 cards, then shuffles both Pokémon back into the deck. This frees Bench space for attackers or support Pokémon and lets search Items rebuild the draw engine after setup (Figure 5).

Figure 5 | Dudunsparce sustains draw and frees Bench space; support Abilities retain their use conditions.

The subsequent 2026 World Championships contextualized these choices: champion Andrew Hedrick used 1 Dunsparce, 1 Dudunsparce, and 4 Crushing Hammer, while semifinalist Brent Tonisson included Moltres. Figure 6 compares their lists with ours using the official decklists.

Figure 6 | Our submission and 2 successful Worlds lists.

Architecture

A Transformer jointly compares the variable number of legal candidates enumerated by the engine, allowing choices to depend on relationships among cards and candidates. Current observations and retained information form the inputs (Table 2). Explicit HP, type, cost, and effect features supplement card IDs to help the network learn common game mechanics across cards with similar properties, aiming to improve generalization with less training data and computation.

Table 2 | Model inputs.

Information	Main features
Cards	ID and category; owner, zone, attachments; current/maximum HP and damage; Special Conditions on Active Pokémon; type, Evolution stage, printed Retreat Cost, Weakness, Resistance, and ex status.
State and legal candidates	Turn and turn owner; deck, hand, Prize, and Bench counts; action usage; request type and count/order constraints; candidate category, owner, and source/target references.
Energy	Effective Energy by type; shortfalls and coverage against printed attack costs; shortfall against printed Retreat Cost.
Attacks and effects	Attack ID, printed damage, typed costs; effect type, amount, targets, and selection conditions for copied attacks and related effects.
Approximate attack outcomes	Estimated remaining HP and Knock Out indicators based on unmodified printed damage and opposing Active HP; target Prize value and separate Weakness/Resistance match indicators.
Effect-resolution state	Remaining damage counters and Energy costs; current attack and user. Phantom Dive uses unallocated counters and target HP.
Actions and public history	Our last 8 decisions; the last 2 completed turns' attacks, users, Prizes taken, and HP changes; current-turn movements, Evolutions, HP changes, and played cards.
Own-deck knowledge	Submitted card counts and remaining deck/Prize counts, with flags indicating whether the remaining counts are known exactly. Full-deck searches update this knowledge; a known card on top of our deck supports effects that use its attacks.

Our policy-value model has 3.23M parameters and is organized into 3 stages (Figure 7).

Encode (Figure 7a) incorporates attachments, pools cards by zone and owner, and adds the corresponding summaries to each card. These enriched vectors form card memory H; global state and history form a shared context.

Figure 7a | Encode. Matching connector labels denote the same information across panels.

Compare candidates (Figure 7b) initializes candidate representations from their source/target card vectors in H, candidate attributes, and context, inspired by AlphaStar's explicit entity and action-argument representations [1]. Candidate representations pass through 3 blocks combining candidate-to-card cross-attention [2], relation-aware candidate self-attention, and an FFN. Shared sources or targets modify attention weights and messages: attaching the same Fire Energy to Dragapult or Moltres creates 2 candidates sharing a source.

Figure 7b | Compare candidates: 3 blocks, 4 attention heads, width 128.

Select (Figure 7c) separates cardinality from contents [3]. The count head uses pre-comparison candidate representations and context to predict k within the current request's bounds. Refined candidate representations determine the contents. An unordered size-k set receives the sum of its candidate scores as its log-score; dynamic programming computes its normalized probability exactly, avoiding enumeration of selection orders for PPO.

Ordered selection instead uses a GRU to update the selection state after each chosen candidate, leaving candidate representations fixed. It makes k selections without replacement or a termination token. The policy returns selected IDs; PPO uses the joint count/content log-probability and a value estimate from context and refined candidate representations.

Figure 7c | Select. Shading and attention weights are illustrative.

Design comparison with BC

We compared architectural variants using BC on decisions from 8,192 self-play games generated by our rule-based Dragapult agent, which reached the gold-medal range midway through the competition. Training and validation were split by game. Validation negative log-likelihood (NLL) and errors on key actions guided adoption. Figure 8 tracks NLL and top-1 across the retained refinements (Table 3). These comparisons assess agreement with teacher actions; playing strength is evaluated separately.

Figure 8 | Development milestones on the same validation games. Left: joint NLL. Right: candidate top-1 on non-forced single selections, independent of count prediction. Seed aggregation and numerical precision differ; adjacent changes are not isolated causal effects.

Table 3 | Retained refinements, in plot order.

Refinement	Change
Read relevant cards	Candidates retrieve observed card information through cross-attention.
Compare candidates	Self-attention incorporates information from other candidates.
Mark shared cards	Candidate relationships modify attention weights and messages.
Distinguish attacks	Add a learned embedding for each attack alongside its numeric ID.
Track deck and Prize counts	Track how many copies of each card remain in the deck and Prizes, and whether those counts are known exactly.
Generalist Reinforcement Learning
Initialization and opponent diversity

BC on game data, including public replays, initialized basic play before PPO self-play (Figure 9). Reaching an attack requires searches, Evolutions, and Energy attachments; BC supplied useful action sequences for PPO to refine. We later distilled a PPO-trained teacher into the revised architecture using approximately 160,000 games, then resumed PPO.

A shared policy played multiple decks on both sides, with increasing emphasis on our submitted Dragapult list.

Opponent decks came from an equal mixture of 2 samplers: uniformly sampled replay records, retaining duplicates to reflect observed frequency; and uniformly sampled archetypes followed by distinct lists within each archetype. This balanced common matchups with coverage of less frequent decks. Deck sampling and opponent-checkpoint selection were separate decisions. Policy weights remained fixed during collection and were updated afterward.

Opponents included current and past checkpoints to help reduce strategy cycling [1]: adapting to the latest opponent can otherwise leave the policy vulnerable to earlier strategies. Only trajectories generated by the current policy entered its PPO update; past checkpoints served solely as opponents.

Figure 9 | Generalist training. The displayed deck and current/past opponent mixture illustrate a configuration; these choices varied across training stages.

Exploration with an entropy bonus

During PPO training, we added an entropy bonus to encourage diverse action choices and gradually reduced its coefficient (Figure 10). A larger entropy-bonus coefficient encourages exploration early; a smaller coefficient later shifts emphasis toward reward maximization. Checkpoint evaluation used greedy play.

Figure 10 | Entropy-bonus annealing. The decreasing coefficient represents the training strategy; landscapes and trajectories are schematic.

Scale and evaluation

Generalist PPO used approximately 273 million games, enabled by the CUDA implementation described below.

We evaluated two aspects of the generalist policy against fixed rule-based agents: deck proficiency and Dragapult performance (Table 4). Deck proficiency refers to how well the policy plays each deck. Figure 11 shows these results for 25 decks. This matters for self-play: a policy that plays Dragapult well may still be a weak opponent when using another deck. We checked results for individual matchups and reviewed leaderboard replays to identify weaknesses that overall win rates could miss.

Table 4 | Generalist policy evaluation.

Evaluation	Setup	Purpose
Deck proficiency	The generalist policy plays different decks against a fixed rule-based Dragapult agent.	Assess play with decks used on the opponent side during self-play.
Dragapult performance	The generalist policy plays our Dragapult deck against multiple fixed rule-based agents.	Monitor overall and per-matchup win rates with our main deck.

Figure 11 | Results for 1 checkpoint playing 25 decks against fixed Dragapult: 100 games per deck, 50 in each seat.

Specialist Reinforcement Learning
Targeting remaining weaknesses

We selected specialist targets using fixed-opponent evaluations, expert replay reviews, and estimated encounter frequency. Fine-tuning inherited the generalist's card usage and setup skills while concentrating PPO games on Dragapult variants, including our submitted list, and selected opponents (Figure 12).

Our reviewers included teammate Miwa Haruki, the 2024 Kyoto Champions League Masters champion who inspired our team name. These reviews distinguished unfavorable matchups from avoidable action choices and translated replay observations into targeted training objectives.

We trained 6 specialists targeting Crustle, Slowking, Alakazam, the Dragapult mirror, Grass Decks(Hydrapple ex, Arboliva ex, and Mega Venusaur ex), and Raging Bolt. Publicly observed opponent cards determine whether to use a matchup specialist or the default policy. All 7 deployed policies play our submitted Dragapult deck.

Figure 12 | Specialist training and routing, schematically. Deployment uses a default policy and 6 matchup specialists, all playing the submitted Dragapult deck.

Slowking: opponent preparation and specialist training

Slowking illustrates why specialization also required competent training opponents. Replay reviews revealed weaknesses against Slowking, a matchup absent from Dragapult's fixed rule-based benchmark. Separately, the generalist won only 9% when playing Slowking against fixed Dragapult (Figure 11), indicating weak opposition for self-play. These observations motivated a 2-stage procedure: strengthen Slowking, then train Dragapult against it.

First, we strengthened a policy that plays Slowking. Features for known cards at the top of its own deck, copied attacks, and targets supported its decisions. PPO initially updated only Slowking against fixed policies playing multiple decks; later training emphasized difficult matchups and Dragapult, mixing fixed opponents with current-policy self-play. In a separate evaluation of 1,000 games against 10 rule-based agents, using fixed seeds, both seats, and greedy play, its win rate increased from 28.3% before PPO to 78.3% at the selected checkpoint.

Second, we trained Dragapult against stronger Slowking opponents. A development curriculum used 4 fixed checkpoints, increasing the strongest opponent's share from 15% to 40%, 65%, and 90%. The submitted anti-Slowking checkpoint came from a separate 2-policy Dragapult–Slowking league.

CUDA Acceleration

To make large-scale self-play practical, we ported game simulation to CUDA. Keeping simulation, feature computation, and policy inference on the GPU accelerates environment updates and avoids repeated observation/action transfers between CPU and GPU memory. Table 5 reports simulation-only throughput with random actions and complete rollout throughput with the trained policy, measured on CPU-only and GPU-only configurations.

Table 5 | Single-device rollout throughput; PPO updates excluded. GPU runs use 1 RTX 5090 with 8,192 environments; CPU runs use an AMD EPYC 7B12.

Measurement	CPU games/s	GPU games/s	GPU configuration
Random actions, environment only	58.72	6,573.8	Legacy environment; no feature computation.
Trained policy	0.535	140.98	Simulation, feature computation, and fp32 inference.

The random-action CPU benchmark used 1 thread and 1 environment over 200 games; the trained-policy CPU benchmark covered 20 games, with thread settings unrecorded.

For end-to-end PPO across 8 RTX 5090 GPUs, a later configuration collected 40,960 games per update and achieved 296.0 games/s against the current policy or 408.8 games/s against past checkpoints. These steady-state rates include collection and PPO updates, excluding initial compilation. CUDA acceleration therefore supported both the generalist's training scale and repeated specialist fine-tuning.

Summary

We combined a flexible Dragapult deck with large-scale self-play, then used matchup evaluations and expert replay reviews to guide specialist training. Strengthening weak training opponents made this additional practice more useful. True to our rhyming team name, we brought Yaruki (motivation), Genki (energy), and Miwa Haruki to the challenge.
