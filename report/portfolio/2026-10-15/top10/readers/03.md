Solution writeup - Team Unown Gradiant - All Decks on Hand

A single self play RL agent, a game theoretic selection system, and lots of training. One policy to play them all.

The Pokémon Company - PTCG AI Battle Challenge Strategy

Hackathon Writeup · Sep 14, 2026

Solution at a glance

In this technical report, I outline my journey participating in the Pokémon TCG challenge. My final submissions came from a single pure self-play RL run: a memoryless 4.4M parameter model trained for 6B steps, the model learns to play roughly 500 decks. The deck pool came from ladder scraped decks, tournament lists, manual creations, and decks developed with a custom LLM harness.

Selecting decks to submit was the most fun. My final submissions were selected based on the choices against the decks top teams were using, applying iterated round robin selection on deck vs deck win rates of my self-play agent.

Quick note on LLM usage disclosure, all competition code was written using Claude models through a single $100 subscription, mostly for coding the ideas on architecture and training. I worked on the competition for approximately 25 days with limited compute, so I didn't spend a lot of time optimizing hyperparameters.

Model architecture

The model uses custom card and action encodings with a four-layer attention network. It scores the engine’s legal actions through a pointer mechanism, making multiselect choices autoregressively.

The network is a memoryless, 4 layer, 8 head, pointer attention model of token embedding dimension 256 (32x8). An external information tracker carries forward facts revealed during play; for example, searching the full deck lets us deduce which cards are prized. These deductions become part of the next observation.

I spent a few days playing the game for the first time, to understand the cards and game dynamics. So that I could make informed choices on encoding the information in the cards, the abilities, damage, hp and other numbers, game state, and lingering effects. All information on each card on the board is organized to a single token encoding. Since card text is in natural language, I also encoded each card's text with a large text embedding model (OpenAI text-embedding-3-large), and PCA'd them down to a small embedding size, this is concatenated with the token encoding.

Avoiding memory in the neural net was deliberate, it usually makes RL training easier and less hyperparameter sensitive, and my instinct was that explicit tracking would be sufficient for Pokémon TCG.

Also, a training-only "oracle" critic receives hidden information not available to the policy, to reduce value-estimation variance and improve PPO training.

PPO Training

I chose to use PufferLib because training speed and cost mattered. After a week of PyTorch experiments on a single RTX 4080 reached 1100+ on the ladder, I used an coding assistant to port the environment and model to PufferLib’s native C/CUDA backend. This substantially improved throughput and enabled multi-GPU training.

The training recipe was PPO with terminal rewards. Most settings stayed close to the defaults; the learning rate schedule and higher batch size with gradient accumulation was particularly useful for convergence. While most hyperparameters were left as default, I've noted the ones I modified in the diagram below.

One surprise was a bug in Pufferlib's native Muon implementation: 1-D parameters such as LayerNorm gains and biases took unbounded momentum steps without weight decay. A run collapsed after roughly 1B steps. Fixing this prevented the same failure in later runs. An important lesson that RL training bugs can take a long time to become obvious. I also added some KL and value loss guards to the steps after this.

Since I do not have access to a high amount of compute, optimizing the cost was important to me. The final run used a rented 8×3090 node at about $1.10/hour, reaching roughly 17K SPS. The 6B step run took around four days and cost about $110; total rented compute for the competition was approximately $350. 3090s are the most cost effective for such small model FP32 RL training, although finding available eight-GPU nodes took some searching.

Local Evaluation

How to evaluate a training checkpoint against so many possible opponents? I don't think there is a generalized foolproof method, but I approximated this with a fixed battery of six diverse decks: Grimmsnarl, Garchomp, Alakazam, Festival Grounds, Dragapult and Crustle. These decks covered varied strategies, from fast aggression, high draw counts, defensive tanks, and stadium effects, all piloted by a fixed checkpoint from an older 1.5B step run. Mean win rate against this battery provided a consistent measure of training progress.

The deck-vs-deck matrix also identified strong candidates and matchup weaknesses, which helped in deck improvements.

In hindsight, the 1.8M parameter model may have been sufficient, while training at roughly 25K SPS on the same hardware.

Self play and league training

Pokémon TCG has strongly non-transitive matchups: a deck can counter one opponent and be poor against most others. Non-transitivity is a common weakness of pure self-play. It needs a diverse opponent pool. Luckily a broad deck pool provided much of that diversity naturally, so self play is sufficient. This is also one of the reasons I chose not to pursue behaviour cloning, as I can only reliably do behavior cloning on a small set of decks. All decks were sampled uniformly while training on the final run.

Ladder and historical tournament lists gave me a starting pool, which I rebalanced a few times by archetype (where an archetype is loosely defined by the major cards that form the backbone of the deck). The pool could also be updated during training, allowing newly scraped or created decks to enter immediately. I wasn’t sure this would work smoothly, but the agent generally picked them up within roughly 100M steps of training after it was added.

Short league experiments against training older checkpoints showed little benefit. On the other hand, they reduced training speed, since self-play gives useful rollouts from both seats, while a frozen opponent gives only one. The final run therefore used 98% self-play, with a random opponent in the remaining games to expose the agent to cards outside training pool (the 500 decks do not span the whole card list).

Known gaps and failed experiments

Slowking line weakness: Observation encoding for Pokémon TCG is tricky, and I went through three iterations. Yet, an observation imperfection weakened play for and against slowking. The tracker misses known top of deck information and whether the opponent placed a card on top.

MCTS inference: Value-based MCTS was promising early on, but gains vanished beyond 1B training steps. I suspect partial observability of limits search effectiveness, even across hundreds of states. Final submissions run pure policy head with argmax.

Reward shaping: Pokémon TCG has a complex rule space, so I was skeptical of the many dense reward-shaping ideas suggested by LLMs. I tried prize-card rewards, but abandoned them after inspecting the learned policies and seeing poor performance. Terminal reward proved to be the right choice.

Deck preparation with value function: I tested "hypothetical draws", replacing the drawn card and using the value function to evaluate a new card. It performed poorly, often favoring illegal or useless combinations, such as two ACESPECs or Stage 1 Pokémon without their basics. This may stem from the generalist agent learning all decks simultaneously.

Anchor Training: Giving higher weightage in training to submission candidate decks towards the end of training, generally this was of no help or detrimental.

Deck creation with LLM loop

Scraping decks from Kaggle ladder isn't sufficient, as proven by top meta moving rapidly over the weeks. Manually creating decks was fun, but I also wanted a generalizable and automated system to create new decks. Experiments on value function iteration and genetic algorithms did not yield good decks.

The promising direction was using LLMs for idea generation. I designed a harness looks at my self-play deck matrix, finds weaknesses, and plays games to find out the weakness. Then the LLM proposes card swaps to close the weakness, and plays with the RL agent to verify, and uses an adversarial reviewer LLM.

Looking at the agent’s traces also gave me ideas for manual creations. I ran this only the last week of the competition, and added 50 new decks to the training pool.

What is the optimal deck choice?

Self-play produced a diverse deck-vs-deck win-rate matrix. Average and worst-case win rates against the training pool gives an initial shortlist, but optimal submission choice depends on the expected field.

A knockout tournament punishes a single bad matchup; on the ladder, weaknesses matter in proportion to how often those opponents appear. The Kaggle ladder has no knockouts, so the useful question was which deck would do best in a round-robin against the decks the other top teams will likely submit? Weaknesses matter in proportion to how often that opponent appears.

Using the deck matrix, I can simulate a candidate’s matchup win rates and vary the different possible scenarios of final ladder mixtures. I started with the observed field, ranked candidate decks against it, then shifted the field toward decks that performed well and see how the ranking changed. I treated this as a sensitivity check on the shortlist, also accounting for the Dragapult bias among the top teams.


Screenshot from my UI to iterate on the deck matrix and ladder preferences

The key uncertainty was whether matchups measured with my agent transferred to other teams. Looking at the games after the submission freeze, comparison against the final top 25 teams suggested the matrix broadly captured matchup structure. These averages include other teams’ submissions, so the differences can also occur due to worse piloting, yet its close on many of the matchups.

Every percentage in the participant table is measured from my Hydrapple submission’s perspective. Against Luca, I won 32.7% versus an expected 48.4%; against several lower-ranked Dragapult opponents, I won roughly 65–67% versus an expected 48–50%. Results against similarly rated participants were generally closer to predictions, though small game counts makes it less conclusiveness.

Final deck selections

Hydrapple ex was my confident first pick. For the second, I wanted a deck that could counter the many teams favouring Dragapult ex

The combination of Hydrapple ex, Teal Mask Ogerpon ex, Meganium and Forest of Vitality produce massive attack and energy additions, making this deck a versatile and aggresive mover. Support pokemon like Fezandipiti ex and Meowth ex help with getting the cards rapidly. Specific cards like Tapu Bulu is good against Crustle, while Judge is a hand disruptor countering draw heavy decks.

I went with Crustle for the Dragapult matchup, blocking damage from ex pokemmon, while Mist Energy protects the attached Pokémon from Phantom Dive’s damage counter placement. This made it an appealing bet against a field heavy on Dragapult.

I was less certain about this second pick, choosing between Crustle and Mega Lucario ex. I had briefly tested both decks on the ladder and both had passed 1200 ELO, but I personally favoured Crustle.

Is the gameplay optimal? Far from it. On the last day I doubted my latest checkpoints were overfitting on strong opponents and losing more to weaker opponents. I chose to submit Crustle with a checkpoint trained upto 4.5B steps, as opposed to Hydrapple with 5.8B step checkpoint. Post freeze analysis revealed this doubt was unfounded, I would have been better off submitting the Crustle deck with the final checkpoint. But the models do have some occasional out of distribution poor gameplay against weaker opponents.

Closing thoughts

Despite being a Pokémon fan and having played the console games in the past, this was my first time playing the TCG. Thank you to The Pokémon Company and Kaggle for this competition, it was both fun and a great learning experience. I had some past RL experience, which helped me make informed choices in the limited time.

I’m excited to see how multi-deck self-play and automated deck creation generalize to future card pools. I’m releasing the training code, decklab harness, and my final submission's code and weights. Hope they’re useful to the broader Pokémon community.
