[4th] A Simple Learning Loop for a Complex Card Game

Teaching a Transformer to play Pokémon TCG through self-play.

The Pokémon Company - PTCG AI Battle Challenge Strategy

Hackathon Writeup · Sep 13, 2026

Playing the Pokémon TCG well requires understanding card interactions, anticipating hidden cards and tracking the opponent's remaining resources. We built a Transformer-based agent that learns these decisions through self-play, using a general model for all decks.

We keep the learning loop simple: reinforcement learning via synchronous PPO. Our main design choices concern how the model represents the game and which experience it learns from.

Representing the Game

With no prior knowledge of Pokémon TCG, we did the obvious: watched YouTube. The way players organized their board offered a useful starting point: earlier evolutions stacked beneath a Pokémon, Energy and Tools attached to it, markers recording states like confusion and dice counting damage. Our representation follows the same idea: each card identity and categorical feature has a learned vector, or embedding. For a Pokémon in play, we add the embeddings of its cards, owner, board location and conditions, then add a learned encoding of numerical features such as HP and damage counters. The result is one token representing that Pokémon in its current state. Other tokens describe cards in hand, discard piles and other visible zones, while a global token carries turn information and resource counts.

The current board does not tell the whole story. A separate, deterministic tracker handles the bookkeeping from our decklist, observations and logs: cards known to remain in our deck or prizes, our known top card, and the revealed portion of the opponent's decklist, including cards in their hand. Additionally, we track last attack, used-Ability markers and summaries of recent actions. This provides the necessary historic context for the otherwise memoryless model. Each tracked card and ability is encoded as another token. Learned scratch tokens provide extra space for combining information.

At each decision point the engine provides a full list of legal actions. Both their number and meaning can change. Each becomes an option token describing the action and the cards involved. Separate tokens identify the effect and its context. Take, for example, Handheld Fan, which moves an Energy from the attacker to an opposing Benched Pokémon. When choosing the destination, the Fan is the effect, the Energy is the context, and the opponent's Benched Pokémon are the options.

The model typically receives 90–150 tokens per decision. A mid-game example might contain 120: 50 describing the visible state, 55 carrying tracked information, 8 representing legal options and 7 special or scratch tokens.

We use a pre-LayerNorm Transformer with bidirectional attention so each option can be evaluated against the relevant cards and tracked information, even as the available choices change. The model scores each option alongside a learned STOP token. STOP means “finish selecting”: for an “up to three cards” decision, the agent can choose two cards and then STOP. During training, we sample choices and during evaluation we choose the greedy action.

A separate value token feeds a critic head, which predicts a categorical distribution over loss, draw and win.

Learning Through Self-Play

Our learning loop uses Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), with the policy playing both sides in parallel self-play games. Rewards come only from the game result: +1 for a win, 0 for a draw and −1 for a loss, without discounting future rewards.

We use the critic's expected value, P(win) − P(loss), to bootstrap short rollouts and generalised advantage estimation (GAE) to assign credit. The critic is trained with cross-entropy against TD(λ) targets. Both GAE and critic targets follow both players' decisions. When the acting player changes, we flip the signs of scalar values and advantages and swap loss and win probabilities.

We smooth model weights with an exponential moving average (EMA) and periodically test the resulting model against a retained champion. Successful candidates replace the champion. A small KL penalty discourages drift from the champion, while an entropy bonus encourages exploration.

Choosing What to Practise

One important missing ingredient is deck lists. We wanted to avoid building decks from scratch as it would add another layer of complexity. Luckily there are many decks freely available: 2,200 tournament deck lists and a growing list of decks used on Kaggle formed our starting pool.

Not knowing the meta, we started training on all decks. After a few days, we saw that some decks were easier for the model to play, while others kept improving with more training. We chose Dragapult for its multi-prize turns and variety of builds, which offered room to improve and adapt to the meta.

Our starting pool covered 464 of the 1,267 most used cards. Over 800 cards therefore remained unseen in training, leaving us exposed to niche strategies. To guard against this, we created 381 "coverage decks" containing rarely played Pokémon and their evolution lines, as well as missing Trainer cards.

To test whether coverage data helps against unfamiliar decks, we trained an opponent on Mega Dragonite ex, an archetype absent from the earlier training data. With roughly 40M samples, it already challenged our heavily trained Dragapult agent. We compared two further training runs: one used ~100M non-coverage samples, while the other used 81M coverage samples. Against Dragonite, their respective win rates were 76% and 88%. The coverage-trained agent also stopped making the unhelpful switches with Boss's Orders that we observed in the non-coverage run.

How Much Do Other Matchups Help?

Coverage helps the policy handle unfamiliar opponents. We next ask whether extensive training on other matchups also improves a matchup it already practises. This matters when one policy plays both sides: focusing on Dragapult must still leave it with capable opponents. We compare specialists and generalists with similar experience in the tested matchup; the generalists also receive much more training elsewhere. For each Dragapult comparison, both policies start from the same checkpoint. We also compare a mirror-only Alakazam policy trained from scratch with a generalist of the same size.

Unless stated otherwise, local evaluations use 5,000 matches with randomized shuffles, balanced seat assignments, and matched seeds across the models or decks being compared.

The focused policies hold up well despite seeing far less training data. Against N's Zoroark, the focused policy reaches 52.1%, compared with 56.0% for the broader policy. The Slowking and Alakazam comparisons are close to even.

Mixed training could hide useful transfer if updates from different matchups interfere. We therefore also pretrain on Alakazam–Mega Lucario ex for different durations, then fine-tune on the Alakazam mirror. This keeps the deck familiar while separating the matchup updates. Longer pretraining gives an early advantage, but the curves largely converge with further mirror training.

Together, these experiments suggest that experience in the matchup itself matters much more than the total training budget.

Adapting the Training

Training ran over four weeks using mostly eight RTX5090s. In total the model consumed 16B samples: 12B Dragapult and 4B non-Dragapult early on.

We weight training matchups according to the field we expect to face. We adjust the mix when comparison with human results reveals weaknesses, and make smaller changes based on daily Kaggle replays.

All matchups improved substantially with one exception: Slowking. It had a favourable matchup against Dragapult in human tournaments, yet won only about 20% when our policy played it. Its strategy relies on preparing the top card of the deck so Seek Inspiration can use a powerful attack. For example, Academy at Night can place Kyurem there from hand, enabling Trifrost. The agent failed to learn this strategy through random exploration, so Dragapult faced a weak opponent. A short, two-epoch behavioural-cloning pass on ~1000 Kaggle replays demonstrated the missing sequence; its play strength made a huge jump. We then trained a specialist through the same PPO process focused on Dragapult–Slowking games. The submitted agent begins with the general policy and switches permanently after observing a Slowking-line card plus supporting evidence such as Academy at Night. The trigger uses only revealed card identities.

Choosing the Final Deck

We shortlist promising decks from ~3,400 tournament and Kaggle lists using their training results. A rule-based generator adds further candidates by keeping the archetype’s core cards fixed and varying flex slots, such as Stadiums and Energy acceleration. We evaluate candidates in self-play against the expected meta, using successive halving to narrow the pool.

The top-performing decks were all familiar Dragapult ex builds with four Crushing Hammers. Their plan is to slow the opponent down while developing attackers and spreading damage across the opponent's board, setting up multi-prize turns.

Drakloak helps find cards and evolves into Dragapult, whose attacks pressure the Active Pokémon and spread damage counters across the Bench. Munkidori moves damage counters from our Pokémon to opposing targets. The Stadium package reinforces this pressure. Two copies of Team Rocket's Watchtower disable Abilities on Colorless Pokémon, disrupting common setup engines while leaving Dragapult and Munkidori active. One copy of Risky Ruins places two damage counters on Basic non-Darkness Pokémon when they are Benched, advancing our multi-prize damage plan and seeding damage on our own board for Munkidori to move. Budew's attack puts the opponent under item lock, while Crushing Hammer can slow their energy progression. Our ACE SPEC, Unfair Stamp, and a single Judge reset hands to interrupt the opponent's plans and make it harder to respond.

We compare alternatives using Chi-Yu as a non-ex attacker exploiting Crustle's Fire weakness, Dudunsparce ex to attack through its protection, or Shaymin to protect the Bench against Slowking. Each improves its target matchup at the expense of others. Our specialist already handles Slowking well, and we choose the list below for both entries based on its highest expected win rate in our testing.

Tournament Results

Our two Dragapult submissions placed our team flg fourth in the final standings. The final rankings can swing with a few lucky matchups or unlucky draws, so we also look at average hourly rank and Bradley–Terry ratings for a steadier picture. Averaging both submissions puts us second on mean Bradley–Terry rating and mean hourly rank. We also remain in the team top ten throughout all measured hourly snapshots.

To put our matchup results in context, we compare leaderboard win rates with human tournament results. Slowking stands out: the demonstrations and specialist training helped turn a weakness into a strength.
Ogerpon/Meganium remains the clearest weakness, receiving relatively little training despite appearing frequently on the leaderboard. This pattern is consistent with our experiments on the value of focused matchup experience.

Conclusion

Our results show that a simple learning loop can be competitive in a complex card game. Focused practice builds matchup strength, while coverage and capable self-play opponents help prepare the agent for a broader field.

Our transfer experiments don't cover all matchups and may not generalise to every deck or stage of learning. For further training, we would prioritise weaknesses in common matchups like Ogerpon/Meganium. Earlier exposure to strong Slowking play might have reduced the need for a specialist.
