[10th Place] From Imitation to RL with Dragapult

Multi-Stage PPO with Identity-Conditioned Imitation Learning

The Pokémon Company - PTCG AI Battle Challenge Strategy

Hackathon Writeup · Sep 14, 2026

fullscreen
Introduction

If you're short on time, here's a quick summary of the pipeline that got me 10th place in Simulation:

I trained one network to imitate hundreds of competition agents, using player identity to learn the style of each agent. I evaluated these imitated agents in tournaments and used the results to filter the replay data. I then continued training with PPO self-play across a broad range of decks. A separate deck tournament helped me narrow the training pool and choose a deck built around Dragapult. In the final stage, one side always used that deck, and the model learned both to play it and to play against it.

If I haven't lost you yet, let's look at how it all works.

Architecture

The network has three parts: a board encoder, an option encoder and an autoregressive action decoder. It chooses from the legal options the simulator provides.

The input starts as a table with one row per card. Unrevealed opponent cards stay marked as unknown. Each row becomes a 128-dimensional vector, or token. I build its base from the card's identity embedding, printed properties and features describing its attacks and abilities. Effect types and target conditions are encoded as categorical features; costs and effect values are numeric. I group these features by attack or ability, project them into vectors and combine them with the card embedding. Different cards can then share features for the same mechanics.

I initialize card, attack and ability embeddings from their text descriptions so cards absent from the replay data still have meaningful representations.

I then add the card's current state: its location, condition and active modifiers. Numerical values and flags pass through learned projections before being added to its token. Player-wide restrictions are encoded in separate player tokens. Effects still being resolved contribute their source, participating cards and resolution order to the game and player tokens.

To imitate different agents with one network, I also turn the team and submission IDs into an input token. The small branch that creates it is the identity head: it tells the model whose decisions to reproduce. This branch is disabled for RL.

The board encoder processes these tokens together with explicit links for attachments, evolution and cards involved in resolving effects. Learned layers pass information between linked cards before each attention layer processes the whole board.

The option encoder reuses the board encoder's output: each legal choice combines its request type with the vectors of the cards it involves. These vectors already account for the cards' state, effects and relationships. Option tokens attend to the board and to one another.

The decoder builds the answer autoregressively, choosing one option at a time. It uses cross-attention to read the encoded options and causal self-attention to account for the choices already made. Each selection is fed back into the decoder before it scores the next choice. Masks prevent duplicate selections, and a stop token ends the answer once the simulator's selection rules allow it.

Imitation

The public replays contain much more than a record of which attack an agent used. They capture the smaller decisions that make a turn work: what to search for, what to discard, where to attach Energy and which targets to choose. I trained the model to reproduce these decisions from the information available when they were made.

Learning from many competitors creates a problem: they do not all play the same way. Different agents can favour different choices, and successive submissions from one team can change their behaviour. Without identity information, the model has to explain those differences from the board state alone.

The identity head gives it the missing context. Team and submission embeddings tell the network whose behaviour it should reproduce, while the rest of the model shares knowledge across players. Submissions belonging to the same team start with nearby embeddings and can diverge during training. Removing this head reduced imitation accuracy by approximately 10% in my experiments.

I first trained the model on replays from submissions made on or after July 1, with at least 40 recorded games and a win rate above 45%.

To decide whose replays to keep, I ran a round-robin championship between the agents the model had learned to imitate. Both sides used the same trained network, each with a different submission identity and a deck from that submission's replays.

I ranked these imitated agents with TrueSkill, removed the lower-ranked submissions' replays from the training set and trained again. I repeated the training, championship and filtering a few times.

The final training set contained 9.9 million decisions from 448 submissions across 67 teams. I kept both wins and losses from these submissions.

During training, I averaged the prediction loss within each decision so that choosing several cards did not automatically receive more weight than choosing one. Submissions with fewer recorded games received stronger label smoothing. Validation used held-out games, with both perspectives of a match kept out of training whenever that match belonged to validation.

RL
Starting from imitation

I started PPO from the imitation checkpoint and added a value head to estimate game outcomes. The model played against itself, and I used both players' decisions to train it. Identity conditioning was disabled. Rewards came from the final result, without additional rewards for damage, Prize cards or particular plays.

Training ran on 8x5090 GPUs. Each cycle collected 98,304 new decisions per GPU for one PPO training pass. The shared settings were a discount factor of 0.999, GAE lambda of 0.9, and policy and value clipping of 0.2. I used AdamW and Muon, reducing their learning rates in later stages.

Deck mutation. Before each training game, a sampled deck could be mutated with the probability set for that stage. To mutate a deck, I first drew how many cards to replace: start at one, then add another with 95% probability, stopping at 60. I picked that many cards at random and sampled their replacements from the simulator's full catalog. Each replacement had to keep the deck legal; otherwise, I tried another card. This produced both small variations and substantially different decks throughout training.

Validation. Every 100 cycles, I evaluated an exponential moving average of the model weights (EMA decay 0.99) against 16 fixed opponents from the frozen imitation model, each with its own submission identity and deck. Every evaluation used the same opponents and match schedule across all 313 candidate decks, even after the training pool became smaller. This meant 20,032 games per checkpoint, including 64 with the chosen Dragapult deck, evenly split between going first and second.

Deck selection. To decide which decks to keep for later stages, I ran a separate championship with the same policy playing every deck. After each stage, I removed the weaker decks and played more games with those that remained to get a more reliable ranking. Each deck played equally often as the first and second player. These rankings helped me narrow the training pool and choose the final deck.

Stage 1 — Broad deck variety

This stage ran for 1,000 cycles. At the start of every training game, I sampled a deck for each player from the pool of 313 base decks. Each deck had a 90% chance of being mutated before play.

For the first 100 cycles, only the new value head trained; the imitation policy stayed frozen. I then unfroze the policy, including its embeddings. Peak learning rates were 0.0004 for AdamW and 0.002 for Muon.

Stage 2 — More time on established decks

I continued from Stage 1 for another 1,500 cycles with the same 313-deck pool, but reduced mutation probability to 10%. Most games now used the original decks, with occasional mutations preserving some variety.

I froze the embedding tables for this and all remaining stages, while the rest of the network continued learning. Peak learning rates were halved to 0.0002 for AdamW and 0.001 for Muon.

Stage 3 — A stronger training pool

After Stage 2, I used the deck championship results to reduce the training pool from 313 to 176 decks. Both players still sampled from this pool, so the model trained across competitive matchups before focusing on one deck. Mutation probability remained at 10%.

I trained for 600 cycles, lowering the peak learning rates to 0.00005 for AdamW and 0.00025 for Muon, with no learning-rate warm-up. The embedding tables stayed frozen.

I scraped the leaderboard and collected the decks used by the top 20 players. I then ran a separate championship between the top 20 players' decks, with my trained model playing both sides of every game. Dragapult had finished fourth in the broader deck championship, but ranked first in the championship restricted to the top 20 players' decks. That is why I chose it for the final self-play stage.

Stage 4 — Focus on the final deck

One player used the chosen Dragapult deck in every game, with its seat randomized. The other player sampled from the remaining 175 decks. Mutation probability was 0% for Dragapult and 10% for the other player's deck. Both players still used the same learning model, so it learned to play Dragapult and to play against it.

I kept the Stage 3 learning rates and frozen embedding tables, and selected the checkpoint after 1,500 cycles of this stage.

Evaluation results
End of training stage	Win rate across all decks	Win rate playing Dragapult
Stage 1	47.4%	54.7%
Stage 2	56.6%	70.3%
Stage 3	57.7%	60.9%
Stage 4	56.0%	79.7%

The final checkpoint won 51 of the chosen deck's 64 games: 26 when going first and 25 when going second. Its result across all decks was slightly lower than Stage 3, while the chosen deck's result was higher. The 64-game result is also a small sample against a fixed development opponent pool, rather than a general win-rate estimate.

Final

The final agent pairs the Stage 4, cycle-1500 policy with the selected Dragapult deck. The deck gives it several competing priorities: develop its evolution line, disrupt the opponent with Budew's Item lock, and coordinate Dragapult's spread damage with Munkidori's damage-counter movement.

This approach helped my agent stay near the top of the leaderboard throughout the competition.
