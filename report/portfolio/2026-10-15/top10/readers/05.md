## 原文正文

The Deck Is Half the Game

A Slowking agent: behavioural cloning, a self-play league, per-matchup specialists, and a Gumbel search rebuilt for hidden information.

The Pokémon Company - PTCG AI Battle Challenge Strategy

Hackathon Writeup · Aug 24, 2026

Why I entered

When I was a kid, I had one question I could never answer: what is the ultimate deck, the one to rule them all? The question is ill-posed, but the real obstacle is simpler: comparing two decks means playing them, well, thousands of times each. This competition made that possible, so thank you to the organisers for an engine, a card pool, and a quarter of a million replays.

1. What makes this game hard

The difficulty is not depth: branching is small, around six options, and the horizon is short. The real difficulty is that you cannot fully see the state, so every evaluation scores a belief, not a position. And there is no fixed board: each player brings 60 cards from a pool of 1,267, so the agent is not learning one game, it is learning matchup A-vs-B.

2. Giving the network everything it needs to see

A network cannot learn what it cannot see. The encoder's job is to carry every strategically relevant fact and route it to the head that needs it.

The engine's observation covers most of it, but a few facts are computed internally and never serialized, the clearest being Premium Power Pro (+30 damage for Fighting Pokémon this turn). Those facts are recovered from the engine's event log and stamped onto the observation. A few gaps remain, all of low strategic impact.

Carrying a fact is not enough: it should reach the network the way a strong player thinks about it.

In practice:

Board and hand are one unordered sequence. Bench position and hand order carry no meaning, so the eighteen board slots and the hand share one permutation-invariant attention stack (every card attends to every other), with flags for what does matter: Active, side, board-or-hand.

Cards carry their evolution ceiling. A benched Duraludon and a Zamazenta look alike but are nothing alike, because one becomes Archaludon ex. So each card is encoded with the encoding of what it becomes: obvious to a human, and something the network would otherwise have to rediscover.

Multi-picks are a sequence, not independent choices. About 4.9% of decisions ask for a subset, e.g. discard three cards, where the second discard depends entirely on the first. So they are decoded autoregressively, each step conditioned on what is already picked.

3. Which decks to train on

The training pool decides both what the agent can pilot and what it can beat.

The metagame is smaller than it looks

The corpus is severely imbalanced: Marnie's Grimmsnarl alone is 35% of all seats, so uniform sampling would give an agent superb against one deck and helpless against the rest. It is also small: of 1,267 cards, only 205 appear in at least 1% of decklists, which makes a restricted training pool affordable.

To sample fairly, decks are grouped into archetypes: two lists match within one Pokémon-name change. That threshold collapses variants while keeping distinct strategies apart (Dragapult/Dusknoir, pure Dragapult and Dragapult/Blaziken stay three archetypes); at two, unrelated lists chain through intermediates.

Cards the network has never learned

Everything outside the pool maps to one shared UNKNOWN embedding, and during training 1% of known cards are masked to it, so the agent learns to play against cards it cannot identify. Only the learned embedding folds: an unknown Pokémon still arrives with its real stats, losing just its text effect.

Two sources of truth

Archetypes came from two sources.

From Kaggle replays: decks with a strong (player, deck) win rate, since a deck winning in one strong pilot's hands proves a high ceiling; and decks with a large share of play, since we will meet them constantly.
From human tournaments (limitlesstcg TEF-POR): winning a real tournament is the same proof of ceiling.

The pool is split into tiers drawn at fixed rates, and near-duplicates cannot both sit in the top tier, so one three-variant family cannot take three slots.

4. Learning to play

Behavioural cloning

Training starts with 100,000 gradient steps of imitation on the replays, so RL begins from plausible moves rather than noise.

League PPO

Training used an AlphaStar-style league: a main agent playing an archive of its own past snapshots, which stops regression, plus a main exploiter finding live holes in the policy and forcing them shut. The league exploiter was probably unnecessary: rock-paper-scissors lives at the deck level here, not in play style.

How far credit should travel (λ = 0.95). Credit decays per decision, and at ~10.8 decisions a turn, 0.95 spans this turn and the opponent's reply, no further: past that the game is decided by cards nobody has drawn yet. It beat λ = 1.0 in every matched comparison we ran.

One network cannot play every deck

This result changed the shape of the project. Slowking has the highest human pilot win rate on Kaggle, yet our generalist won only 48% of its games with it. The hypothesis: archetypes compete for the same weights. The shared blocks that let board reading transfer across decks also let conflicting policies interfere, and Slowking's plan is unlike anything else in the format.

The test: pin a copy of the generalist to one archetype and keep training against the field. Slowking went 47.6% → 72.1% (+24.5 pp). Four other archetypes, starting near 58–60%, gained +4.6 to +7.0 pp.

Specialising against the opponent

If one network cannot pilot every deck, can one play against every deck? Same experiment one level down: fork the Slowking expert into twelve matchup specialists. The gains are second-order: +3.5 pp on average.

At play time, each archetype group is identified by its core Pokémon: as soon as the opponent reveals cards consistent with exactly one group, the agent switches to that specialist, otherwise it stays on the generalist.

Everything converged faster than expected

The ceilings arrive early: behavioural cloning stops improving at about 100k gradient steps, the generalist league at 2M games, fine-tuning to one deck at 500k, fine-tuning against one opponent at 200k. We trained past several of those and got nothing for it.

At rented-GPU prices that is around $70 for the whole pipeline.

5. Search at test time
What search is actually for

Two things: lookahead, and patching what the network cannot know (effects the encoding omits and cards outside the training pool with no learned prior).

Search simulates, observes the consequence, and corrects, worth +6.4 percentage points of win rate against the archetype pool (0.71 → 0.77).

Gumbel rather than PUCT

PUCT uses the visit count for two incompatible jobs: exploration bonus during search, improved policy afterwards. So we use Gumbel, as DeepMind's own mctx recommends; every comparison we ran agreed.

Dealing the opponent

Simulating requires dealing the opponent a deck: revealed cards are matched by Jaccard similarity against the Kaggle replay decklists, and the nearest fills the remainder.

Where MCTS breaks on this game

The game is not zero-sum across information sets. V(us) ≠ −V(them): both players can hold weak hands, so negating a pessimistic opponent's value inflates the move that hands them the turn, and search learns to like "end turn". Fix: only evaluate where it is our turn and our decision.

Plies are not a fair horizon. One turn can be one ply or thirty, so equal-depth candidates can be a full turn apart, and if we are behind, deeper positions look worse, biasing search toward lines that never advance game time. Fix: every simulation restarts from the root and stops at the beginning of our next turn, so all candidates are compared at the same point in game time.

One sampled world lies about chance. A draw effect that hits in the sampled world looks certain when it may be one in three. Fix: the budget goes to breadth: many simulations, each in a fresh world.

These are also why search cannot be the training loop: its output stays noisy, and in AlphaZero that noisy output is the target. I spent the first half of the competition there; it reached ~20% against public 850-Elo bots, at hundreds of engine steps per decision against one for PPO.

6. The deck: Slowking

Why this deck

Its best human pilot won 82.7% over 75 games, the strongest record in the replay pool, and our fine-tuned expert reached 72.1% with it, the highest of any archetype. It is also top-two in human tournaments and dodges the format's counter-cards, Crustle and Cornerstone-Mask Ogerpon. Dragapult was the serious alternative.

The game plan

For two Energy, Slowking's Seek Inspiration uses the attack of the top card of your deck, discarding it.

So the deck is built around that attack:

Control the top card. Academy at Night places one card on top; Ciphermaniac's Codebreaking places two.
Attack. The main line is Kyurem's Trifrost, with Conkeldurr for maximum single-attack damage and Annihilape to reach knockouts otherwise out of range.
Re-arm. Trifrost discards both Energy, but Boomerang Energy returns to the Pokémon that discarded it: one attachment next turn and you attack again. Wondrous Patch plus a manual attachment builds a new attacker in one turn.
Support attackers. Lillie's Clefairy ex is a cheap second attacker whose ability gives every Dragon Pokémon a Psychic weakness, turning Dragapult ex into a one-hit knockout. Smoochum sets up Energy on the bench early. Latias ex gives free switching to everything except Slowking. Mega Kangaskhan ex is by far the best opener: two extra cards a turn.

Every remaining card exists for consistency: search a specific card, draw cards, or recover one from the discard.

Building the list

The starting point was the human consensus list published at limitlesstcg.

First, translate it into the legal format. Human lists run 1.62 Metagross on average (300 damage, 330 with Brave Bangle), which is TEF-CRI while the engine's pool is TEF-POR. That is the biggest structural cost of the restriction: Metagross would have one-shot Dragapult ex, Hydrapple ex and most of the field. The best legal replacement, Conkeldurr, caps at 250, which also makes Brave Bangle worthless: 280 clears nothing extra.

Second, tune it against observed losses. Reviewing leaderboard defeats, the recurring cause was not a missing answer to a specific card; it was failing to assemble the board at all. Three changes followed: −1 Switch, −1 Surfer, −1 Boomerang Energy; +1 Mega Kangaskhan ex, +1 Ciphermaniac's Codebreaking, +1 Basic Psychic Energy.

All of it serves one combo: Codebreaking puts the exact two cards you want on top, and Mega Kangaskhan ex draws them. The deck does not need a quantity of cards but a specific set (Energy, Slowking, Kyurem, Stadium), and missing one means not attacking. That is the general trade: tech cards (Switch, Prime Catcher, Boss's Orders) can unlock an otherwise unwinnable game, but lose the games where they crowded out setup. A/B tested head-to-head at large samples, the consistency builds won.

One change I would still make: put a single Switch back. After attacking, Slowking has no Energy to retreat, so an opponent who declines the knockout and develops instead leaves us pinned with a useless Active.

7. How it holds up across the field

The bad end is narrow.

Alakazam, 19.2%: the one true hard counter. An even prize race, but its single-Energy attacker comes online far faster than our setup, and Shaymin shuts off our bench sniping.
The grass band, 38–46%. High-HP attackers we cannot one-shot, plus a Tera Ogerpon untouchable on the bench, closing the sniping line.
Archaludon, ~50%. Out of one-shot range at 300 HP, and Assemble Alloy re-powers it every turn: they repeat a simple loop while we must play near-perfectly.
8. What I'd do differently

Fine-tuning one deck against a generalist field. The Slowking expert learned to beat decks piloted by the generalist, i.e. by mediocre pilots. The right structure is to fine-tune all archetypes in parallel and have the experts play each other, so every deck is piloted near its ceiling.

Committing to a deck too early. The agent now makes only one or two identifiable mistakes per game, so the remaining headroom is in the deck, not the policy. I should have spent more time choosing before pinning.
