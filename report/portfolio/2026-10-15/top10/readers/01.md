Aim to Be a Pokémon Master

1st-Place Write-up in the PTCG AI Battle Challenge Simulation Track

The Pokémon Company - PTCG AI Battle Challenge Strategy

Hackathon Writeup · Jul 19, 2026

Thank you to the hosts for organizing such a special Pokémon competition during Pokémon's 30th-anniversary year, and to every Pokémon player who made it so much fun to play together. I have many fond childhood memories of Pokémon, and this competition brought back the fun of playing with them.

In this Pokémon TCG AI Battle Challenge, each agent brought a 60-card deck and played games under Pokémon TCG rules. I was fortunate to finish 1st on the simulation track. This write-up describes how to battle together with Pokémon and master the Pokémon TCG with AI agents.

TL;DR. The final submissions use approximately 2.24M-parameter Transformer models, trained through self-play reinforcement learning using PPO across 4 stages with a progressively narrower and more specialized range of decks. Model inputs combine basic card and legal-option information with information about the opponent's deck and hand inferred from the game history. Decks are clustered into archetypes to balance training exposure and help analyze deck strength, and counter relationships. A local arena is maintained to evaluate checkpoint–deck pairs and select the strongest representatives. Promising decks from strong archetypes are selected based on their competitiveness both within and across archetypes, then refined through small card mutations designed to reduce weaknesses against counters while preserving advantages in other matchups. Three fixed-deck specialists were trained: 2 Dragapult ex and 1 Hydrapple ex. The 2 Dragapult ex agents were submitted based on the metagame observed on the final day.

Let's get into the full write-up, which covers game engine and action interface / game representation and feature engineering / model architecture / training framework / local evaluation and deck selection / evaluation phase / closing thoughts. Decks are identified by the first 6 hexadecimal characters of a SHA-1 hash of their 60 card IDs. Complete deck lists are provided in decks.xlsx.

1. Game Engine and Action Interface

Thanks to greySnow1 and c-number2 for requesting access to the engine source and clearer rules, and to the hosts for releasing the official source.3 Early access made RL iteration and engine debugging much faster without reconstructing the engine from replays.

Figure 1 shows the interface between the official C++ engine and the agent. At initialization, the engine asks the agent for a legal 60-card deck. During play, it sends the visible state, request details, and legal options to the agent, which returns the selected option indices.

2. Game Representation & Feature Engineering

This section describes how we turn the information provided by the game engine into model inputs. Information is encoded as different types of tokens, combining the game state provided directly by the engine with information inferred from past actions and game events. Inputs comprise a global token for game-level information and object tokens for cards and legal action options.

2.1 Global Token

Table 1 shows the global-token features by category. Complete definitions and details are provided in features_global.xlsx.

2.2 Object Tokens

Table 2 shows feature groups of the object token. Each token also receives 64-dimensional learnable card and evolution embeddings. The role features indicate the token's role and, when applicable, whether the referenced card belongs to the acting player. Static features describe the basic card information. Zone features summarize all copies of the same card across locations, including the hand, deck, and Bench, for example 3 in the deck and 1 on the Bench. Each card summary token represents 1 distinct card identity, with up to 30 per player. Instance features describe an individual card in play: a Pokémon in the Active Spot or on the Bench, or the Stadium. For Pokémon, they distinguish copies of the same card by damage, attached Energy and Tools, and other current status. Option features describe the legal action, such as an attack, attachment, or card selection. Complete definitions and details are provided in features_object.xlsx.

Object tokens represent cards or legal options and have various types. Own and opponent card tokens summarize each distinct card in our deck or seen or inferred from the opponent. In-play tokens represent individual Pokémon in the Active Spot or on the Bench, plus the Stadium. Context tokens represent the card named by the current request, while effect tokens represent the source card of the resolving effect. Option tokens represent legal choices. Different token types activate different feature groups, with the others set to zero, as shown in Table 3.

Option tokens can involve cards, sometimes more than 1. Which card supplies each group of card-related features depends on the scenario, as summarized in Table 4 below. Equivalent options with the same effect, such as choosing between 2 interchangeable copies of the same card in hand, are merged into 1 option token while retaining their multiplicity.

Table 5 lists the maximum number of tokens of each type supported by this representation.

Autoregressive (AR) selection picks cards one at a time when multiple cards are requested, with STOP available once the minimum is met. Attachment and evolution are also split into 2 picks, such as choosing an Energy and then the Pokémon to attach it to. These picks return multiple option indices for a multi-card selection or 1 index for a card–target pair, with inputs updated after each pick.

4. Training Framework
4.1 Distributed training and simulation infrastructure

Distributed Data Parallel (DDP) training is applied. Game execution and feature calculation run in C++ on the CPU, using the official game engine with a custom batched interface. The model is implemented in JAX.

Training throughput on 4× RTX 5090 GPUs was approximately 40k–60k SPS, corresponding to roughly 220–460 games/s, depending on the machine and the proportion of league matches. Each sample is one policy decision, including each autoregressive step when selecting multiple cards.

4.2 PPO training parameters

Models were trained through self-play reinforcement learning using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE). The table below shows the general training settings. Advantages are normalized within each minibatch. The value head predicts the bootstrapped return using mean squared error with a loss coefficient of 0.5.

4.3 Archetype and deck clustering

The official metagame data contains 3,593 distinct decks. Since replays are public, many participants learn from strong decks and modify them. Some deck archetypes therefore have many more variants than others. E.g., 346 decks contain Dragapult, compared with only 21 containing Zoroark.

To reduce the bias toward archetypes with more variants under uniform deck sampling, decks are hierarchically clustered using static card features and learned embeddings, splitting branches when Pokémon-card or Energy-card cosine similarity falls below 0.5. Decks within an archetype can still differ by 40 cards, so families are formed by assigning decks with the most neighbors first to the nearest seed within 3 replacements. Table 8 shows the clustering results for all decks that reached the top 50.

4.4 Hierarchical training stages

Strong performance across all decks does not necessarily translate into the best play with a specific deck. Training therefore progressed through 4 stages, with an increasingly specialized range of decks.

Stage 1: Broad self-play. Training used a daily refreshed top-50 deck pool supplemented with manually added decks, sampling archetypes uniformly, then families and decks within them according to win-rate-based weights.

Stage 2: Archetype representatives. Self-play focused on each archetype's champion deck from the local arena (Section 5.1), sampled uniformly.

Stage 3: Selected decks. Training continued from Stage 2 on a smaller pool centered on Dragapult ex and Hydrapple ex, reflecting the recent metagame and producing stronger opponents for Stage 4.

Stage 4: Single-deck specialists. Each model trained on one fixed deck against a progressive ladder of frozen opponents from Stages 1–3 and the local arena. After clearing the ladder, opponents against which the learning model had lower win-rate EMAs were sampled more often.

5. Local evaluation and deck selection
5.1 Local evaluation arena

Models were evaluated locally in a continuously running arena with 1 slot for each archetype. Candidates from Stages 1, 2 and 4 were queued by archetype, played 256 games against each other champion, and replaced the incumbent if their mean win rate was higher.

Tables 9 and 10 show the latest arena results before the submission deadline. The Stage 4 single-deck models improved substantially over the less specialized Stage 2 models. For Dragapult, mean win rate increased from 76.2% to 85.7–86.8%, and even the worst cross-archetype matchup exceeded 50%. Hydrapple's mean increased from 78.3% to 83.5%, while its worst matchup increased from 13.3% to 43.4%.

5.2 Deck selection
5.2.1 Deck selection for Stage 4 single-deck training

Before Stages 3 and 4, Hydrapple (a7) and Dragapult (a3) led the arena at 76.3% and 73.0% mean win rate, each with only 1 matchup below 45% (Tables 11–12). Hydrapple played evenly against Dragapult and countered its main weakness, a2, so both advanced to single-deck selection.

Internal round robins identified strong decks within a3 and a7 (Tables 13a–13b), followed by cross-archetype matches between their top 10 decks (Table 13c), with 512 games per pair.

Variants changing fewer than 4 cards were tested from the top 2 a3 decks and the leading a7 deck based on strategy analysis, common card combinations and counter matchups, aiming to reduce their weaknesses while preserving internal and cross-archetype performance. This produced the Stage 4 decks shown in Table 14.

After card replacement and Stage 4 training, f38fb5 reached 95.7% against a2 and f04db8 reached 43.4% against Alakazam. With the same Stage 2 model, 917977 retained similar performance to its parent: 53.7% against a3 and 58.7% against a7. It turns out that Chi-Yu’s Jamming Tower combo benefited from a simulator-specific Knock Out timing discrepancy, confirmed by the hosts.4

5.2.2 The final day

Table 15 summarizes the training history and scale, together with first- and second-player win rates in training games.

Three Stage 4 single-deck models were prepared for final-day submission. Actions are sampled during training, while inference selects the highest-scoring legal option (argmax).

The initial choices were f38fb5 and f04db8. As Alakazam appeared among top teams while a2 was rarely observed, 917977 replaced f04db8 for stronger expected Dragapult matchups.

6. Evaluation Phase

The team finished 1st among 6,807 teams. The 2 submitted agents reached final Elo ratings of 1398.2 for f38fb5 and 1291.1 for 917977. Since Day 2 of the evaluation phase, f38fb5 ranked above every other team for 97.3% of the tracked time, while 917977 independently did so for 54.0%. Both submissions achieved win rates well above the rest of the field against the final top-15/30/50 submissions (Table 17).

Tables 18 and 19 show the 2 submissions' match records against top submissions during the evaluation phase. Both had an advantage against most opposing submissions. Although most counter relationships identified in the local arena were also observed during the evaluation phase, both submissions won only about 50% against top Slowking decks. This approximately reflects the earlier arena analysis of the a3–a12 matchup, where the same a3 deck won 50.8% with a Stage 2 player. A Stage 4 player improved this to 70.3%, but that advantage did not persist as opponents developed stronger models for playing a12 decks.

Around 100 games per opponent are too few for a reliable win-rate estimate. At least 500 games per opponent are needed. With so few games per opponent, I was fortunate to survive the resulting volatility in the Elo ratings and take my Pokémon to the podium with me. 🎉

7. Closing Thoughts
7.1 What didn’t work

Search during training and inference. Neither sufficiently benefited the final approach.

End-to-end deck building. Jointly learning deck construction and play produced a deck with no Energy cards, relying on hand disruption and Energy denial to win by exhausting the opponent’s deck, echoing the deck-out finish in the 2026 World Championships final.5 Against weaker early models, it had an advantage against almost every training archetype except an Iono's Bellibolt ex deck carrying 22 Energy cards, but this advantage faded as opponents improved.

We also tried completing Limitless-seeded decks,6 which still underperformed human-built decks. Two generated decks joined Stage 1–2 training and are marked in Table 9.

7.2 Why not predict the opponent’s cards?

Reasoning about hidden information is central to the game. The model was given only facts and bounds established from observable game history, rather than Bayesian estimates based on familiar decks. Such priors could underestimate unseen cards or strategies, potentially giving opponents outside the prior’s coverage an advantage.

7.3 Anecdotal

Sometimes the agent repeated damage-counter placement requests after they were rejected, leaving the remaining count unchanged and the game stuck. To avoid these loops during training, a player lost after 1,000 decisions.

8. Ending

What an unforgettable Pokémon journey! Looking forward to what comes next.
