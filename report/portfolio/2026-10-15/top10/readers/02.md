2nd Place in Simulation: From 3,341 Decks to One Specialist

From broad self-play to metagame-driven deck selection and specialist training

The Pokémon Company - PTCG AI Battle Challenge Strategy

1. Solution Overview

Our approach combines broad experience against diverse opponent decks with deck selection targeted at leading teams. One shared policy makes it practical to estimate local win rates for many candidate decks without training each candidate's agent separately. Its accumulated experience also provides the foundation for the final specialist.

We first built a pool of 3,341 distinct deck lists from public Kaggle episodes. Using this pool, we trained a generalist from random weights through self-play with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), without gameplay demonstrations (Phase 1 PPO). We then used the generalist to compare candidate decks against those used by leading teams and selected Crustle/Mega Kangaskhan ex. Finally, we continued training the generalist with the selected deck to produce a specialist (Phase 2 PPO). This agent had 2.4 million policy parameters and placed second in the Simulation track.


2. Method
2.1 Deck Pool

We collected deck lists from the public Kaggle episode datasets released during the competition. We removed duplicates, treating lists with the same cards and counts as the same deck. The resulting pool contained 3,341 distinct 60-card decks, providing both training variety and candidates for our final deck selection.

2.2 Policy and Game Representation

We used action-prediction accuracy from imitation learning (IL) on leading teams' episodes, together with execution speed, to guide feature and architecture design. However, IL accuracy alone could not fully predict benefits in reinforcement learning (RL). The deeper-simulation configuration was one example: its benefit emerged only after RL training (Section 3.1). The generalist itself was trained from random weights, without IL pretraining.

The resulting design gives the actor a broad representation of player-visible information, using Transformers to learn relationships among its elements. Inputs cover the public state, its own hand and starting deck, card rules, selection context, recent public actions, and unseen cards from its starting deck. A state Transformer encodes visible entities; separate encoders summarize the state and deck.

The game engine supplies legal options, but their effects may depend on subsequent selections that the option descriptions do not capture.
We therefore simulate each legal option using a modified version of the official C++ game engine. A limited breadth-first search follows subsequent selections and explores public choices. It stops when the opponent must act, unknown information is needed, or a search limit is reached. We summarize changes in damage, healing, Prizes, card movement, and board state across the explored branches. These summaries are the transition features. A shorter follow-up check detects newly available actions, such as an attack enabled by attaching Energy. An option-set Transformer combines these features with the state and deck representations to score all legal options together.


2.3 Phase 1: PPO Across Decks

We trained the generalist with PPO for about 12 billion environment steps. For each game, we sampled both players' decks from the pool and randomized the learner's seat. The learner sampled actions from its policy, while the opponent chose the highest-probability legal action. The only reward was the game result: +1 for a win and -1 for a loss.

Some deck themes were much more common than others. To prevent them from dominating training, we balanced sampling across major themes and reserved a share for less common themes. Within each theme, we favored decks containing cards the policy had seen less often and reduced sampling of decks with consistently low win rates. In 5% of games, we assigned both players the same deck to reduce deck-strength and matchup bias, aiming to make win/loss rewards more informative about play quality.

Opponents used the current policy 80% of the time and a historical snapshot 20%. Snapshots were added every 25 million environment steps and retained for 500 million steps. For each PPO update, we sampled one uniformly from this history.

During training, a critic estimated the expected game result. Inspired by PerfectDou (Yang et al., 2022), we gave the critic additional information about hidden cards and the opponent's starting deck to help it estimate that result. The actor never received these additional inputs.

To make broad training practical, C++ maintained game states, advanced games, and simulated legal options, using OpenMP to parallelize CPU work. It exported numerical feature arrays to PyTorch without JSON serialization. Python coordinated rollout collection, while PyTorch batched policy inference, action selection, and PPO updates on GPUs. Selected actions returned to C++ to advance the games. At measured throughput, 12 billion steps would take about 2.7 days on eight RTX 5090 GPUs.

2.4 Selecting a Deck

We wanted a deck that performed well against decks used by leading teams. A high local Elo across the full deck pool could still hide poor matchups against those opponents. We selected our deck in four steps:

We collected the deck lists used by leading teams near the end of the competition.
We shortlisted candidates from each major deck theme using local Elo measured with the Phase 1 policy.
We evaluated each candidate against the leading teams' decks.
We chose Crustle/Mega Kangaskhan ex based on these win rates, local Elo, and a review of the candidates' strengths and weaknesses against the target decks.

Local deck comparisons used the same Phase 1 policy for both players. All local evaluations used highest-scoring actions and alternating player seats.

Combining overall local Elo with local win rates against leading teams' decks let us select a strong candidate specifically for the matchups we expected near the top of the leaderboard.

2.5 Phase 2: PPO on the Selected Deck

We continued training from the generalist after about 12 billion steps. The learner now always used the selected deck, while opponents continued to use a range of decks. Opponents with different decks mainly used fixed recent Phase 1 policies. Opponents in mirror games used recent learner snapshots. We trained for another 4.2 billion steps with the same PPO optimization settings.

We tracked progress against 23 deck lists collected from the top 30 teams at the time, plus a mirror matchup, using the fixed final Phase 1 generalist as the opponent. Each matchup used 256 games, and win rates were averaged equally across matchups. The mean win rate rose from about 66% early in Phase 2 to 76–77% in evaluations near the submitted checkpoint, an improvement of about 10 percentage points.

2.6 Submission Inference

For each decision, the submitted agent first ran the simulations described in Section 2.2 to build transition features. The actor then scored all legal options in one pass and chose the highest-scoring option. When several items could be selected, it predicted how many to choose and took the highest-scoring items. We also tested critic-based re-ranking and beam search after feature construction. They were slower and did not consistently improve play, so we retained greedy actor selection. The submitted agent did not use the critic.

3. Results and Analysis
3.1 Transition-Feature Comparison

Our other submitted agent used Dragapult ex and depth-1 simulation probes, finishing at about 1,160 Elo. Our Crustle agent used depth 3 with a follow-up check. The two submissions used different decks and slightly different training settings, but the main change to the model was how its transition features were constructed. We therefore compared the two feature configurations during Phase 1 training to assess their effect on play.

Depth 1 simulated only the initial option. Depth 3 followed up to two further selection steps. For example, when playing Boss's Orders, depth 1 stops at the prompt to choose an opposing Benched Pokémon. Depth 3 can explore those choices and record which Pokémon would become Active. These depths count selection steps, not full game turns.

The depth-3 configuration also included the follow-up check described in Section 2.2.

We trained two Phase 1 policies from random weights with the same seed and otherwise identical settings. Both faced the same fixed earlier Phase 1 policy. We selected eight decks, one from each of eight representative deck themes. For each deck, the evaluated policy faced the fixed opponent using that same deck (mirror games) and two other decks (cross-deck games), with 256 games per matchup. Figure 3 averages these 8 × 2 = 16 cross-deck matchups with equal weight.


Over the first 8 billion steps, depth 3 with the follow-up check performed better through most of training. Its cross-deck win rate averaged about two percentage points higher across evaluation checkpoints and peaked at 57.1%, compared with 55.8% for depth 1.

3.2 Local Evaluation versus Public Results

First, we matched public games among the final top-200 participants to deck pairs evaluated locally with 32 games per pair. To reduce the influence of differences in agent strength, both agents had to have Elo of at least 1,100 and be within 50 points of each other. We excluded mirror matchups and pairs with fewer than five public games. This left about 14,500 games across 898 player–opponent deck pairings. Local and public win rates had a modest positive correlation (Spearman ρ = 0.30). Figure 4 shows ten groups of pairings with similar local win rates. It uses Wilson confidence intervals for local results and bootstrap intervals resampling deck pairings for public results.

Second, we compared local and public win rates for our submitted Crustle specialist against 20 distinct opponent deck lists, ranked by the highest submission Elo achieved with each list. The local evaluation used 256 games per deck against the fixed final Phase 1 generalist. The correlation was stronger (Spearman ρ = 0.75), but local evaluation generally overestimated public win rates.

The positive correlations suggest that local evaluation captured useful matchup trends despite differences between the local and submitted policies. This supports using the generalist to shortlist candidate decks against a target field.

3.3 Deck Strategy and Public Results

Our Crustle/Mega Kangaskhan ex deck aimed to survive long enough for the opponent to run out of cards. Crustle's Mysterious Rock Inn prevents attack damage from opposing Pokémon ex. This gave it a defensive role against decks that relied on those attackers. Mega Kangaskhan ex provided card draw through Run Errand and another attacking option. Healing cards such as Jumbo Ice Cream helped keep our Pokémon in play over repeated turns.

In public games against 30 high-ranked active submissions, the submitted specialist recorded 376 wins and 352 losses. Opponents running out of cards accounted for 39% of our wins. Against Dragapult ex, the largest matchup group, 52% of our wins came from the opponent running out of cards.

The deck had a clear weakness: non-ex attackers could bypass Crustle's protection. The difficult Alakazam matchup in both local and public games (Figure 5) was consistent with that weakness. We accepted this trade-off to target the decks we expected to face. However, there was about a day between deck selection and the submission deadline, during which the field could still change. Our result also owed something to luck: decks we struggled against, such as Alakazam, did not become more common during that period.

4. Conclusion

One policy trained across 3,341 decks served two purposes: it let us compare candidate decks, and it provided the starting point for training our final specialist. Public results supported the matchup trends found in local evaluation, while also showing limits in predicting individual win rates.
